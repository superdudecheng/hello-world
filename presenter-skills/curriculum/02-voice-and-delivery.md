# Module 2 — Voice & Vocal Delivery

> *"It's not what you say, it's how you say it."*

Your voice is an instrument. Most people play it on one flat note. Learning to
vary it is one of the fastest, most noticeable upgrades you can make. This module
is about the **six levers** of vocal delivery and how to pull them.

**Watch first:** Julian Treasure, *"How to speak so that people want to listen"*
— he lays out the vocal toolbox better than anyone (see [videos](../resources/videos.md)).

---

## The six levers

### 1. Pace — speed
Nervous speakers rush. Rushing signals anxiety and buries your points. Deliberate
speakers slow down.
- **Slow down overall**, especially at the start (nerves push you fast).
- **Vary the pace:** speed up for excitement and energy; slow *right* down for
  your most important line. Slowness signals importance.

### 2. Pause — silence
**The pause is the single most underused tool in speaking.** Silence feels
eternal to you and powerful to them.
- Pause **before** a key point (creates anticipation).
- Pause **after** a key point (lets it land).
- Pause **instead of filler.** Every "um," "uh," "like," and "so" is a pause
  you're afraid to leave empty. Replace them with confident silence.
- A pause also buys *you* time to think, and slows your racing heart.

> Drill: deliver a sentence, then count "one-one-thousand, two-one-thousand" in
> silence before the next. It'll feel far too long. On video it'll look
> commanding.

### 3. Pitch / prosody — the melody
Monotone kills. Prosody is the rise and fall of pitch that carries meaning and
emotion. Let your pitch move naturally with what you mean. A question rises; a
firm conclusion falls. Read to a child to feel how much range you actually have —
then bring a fraction of that into your talks.

**Watch for "upspeak"** — ending statements on a rising pitch, like a question?
It makes you sound uncertain. Land your statements with a downward pitch.

### 4. Volume & projection
Speak to the person in the **back row.** Projection comes from breath and support
(see below), not from straining your throat. Then use volume as a lever: drop to
near-a-whisper to pull people in; rise to emphasize. Contrast is what commands
attention — not constant loudness.

### 5. Tone & timbre
Timbre is the *richness* of your voice. A warmer, deeper, chest-resonant voice is
perceived as more authoritative and trustworthy. You can develop it: good
posture, relaxed throat, and breathing from the diaphragm all deepen and warm the
voice. Tension raises and thins it.

### 6. Articulation
Crisp consonants make you intelligible and confident. Lazy articulation makes even
great content sound mumbled. Warm up your mouth (tongue twisters) and slightly
over-articulate compared to casual conversation.

---

## Breath is the foundation of all of it

Every vocal lever runs on breath. Shallow, high chest-breathing (the nervous
default) starves your voice and shakes it. **Diaphragmatic breathing** — low,
into the belly — supports volume, steadies the voice, and calms the nervous
system.

**Feel it:** Put a hand on your belly. Breathe so the hand moves out, not your
shoulders up. Speak on the *exhale*, from that supported air. This one habit
underpins projection, steadiness, and calm.

---

## Killing filler words

Filler ("um," "uh," "like," "you know," "so," "basically") comes from a fear of
silence — your mouth keeps running while your brain catches up. The cure is not
willpower; it's **the pause**. Train yourself: when you'd say "um," close your
mouth and be silent instead. Recording yourself is how you catch them — most
people have no idea how often they say "um" until they hear it.

---

## Warm up before you speak — always

Singers and actors never perform cold. Neither should you. A 3-minute warm-up
(see [drills](../practice/drills.md)) makes your voice richer, clearer, and
steadier. Do it in the car, the bathroom, wherever — just do it. It also
discharges nervous energy.

---

## 🎯 This module's exercises

1. **The pause drill:** Record a 1-minute talk. Deliberately insert a 2-second
   silence before and after your key point. Watch it back — notice how it feels
   long to deliver but powerful to watch.
2. **The range drill:** Read a children's story aloud with *huge* vocal
   exaggeration — silly voices, big pitch swings. Then give a talk and keep 25%
   of that range. Record both.
3. **Filler audit:** Record a 2-minute unscripted talk. Count your filler words.
   Re-record replacing each one with a pause. Compare.
4. **Re-record last week's talk** applying pace, pause, and pitch. Compare the two
   versions side by side.
