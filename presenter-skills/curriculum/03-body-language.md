# Module 3 — Body Language & Stage Presence

> *"The body speaks before the mouth does."*

Your audience reads your body before they process your words. Confident,
congruent body language makes them trust you; nervous, closed body language makes
them doubt you — regardless of what you're saying. The good news: presence is a
set of behaviors you can practice.

**The core drill for this whole module:** record yourself and **watch it on
mute.** Everything below becomes obvious when you can't hear the words.

---

## Posture — the root of presence

Everything starts here.
- **Stand tall and grounded:** feet about shoulder-width apart, weight even on
  both feet, spine long, shoulders back and down. This is a stable "home base."
- **Open your torso.** Crossed arms, hunched shoulders, and turning sideways all
  read as defensive or small. Keep your chest open to the audience.
- Good posture doesn't just *look* confident — it *makes* you feel and sound more
  confident (it opens your breathing). See Amy Cuddy's talk in
  [videos](../resources/videos.md) for the presence idea (note the caveat there).

## Hands & gestures — your most visible tool

The universal beginner question: *"What do I do with my hands?"*

- **Gesture naturally and purposefully.** Use your hands to illustrate — show
  size, direction, contrast, sequence ("on one hand… on the other…"). Watch any
  great speaker: their hands are always working.
- **Return to a neutral home base** between gestures — hands relaxed at your
  sides or lightly together at your waist. From there, gesture, then return.
- **Avoid:** hands glued in pockets, gripping the podium, fig-leaf clasping,
  fidgeting with a clicker or ring, or repetitive "karate chops." Fidgeting leaks
  nerves.
- **Bigger rooms need bigger gestures.** Scale your body language to the space.
  What feels big to you reads as normal from row 20.

## Eye contact — the connection engine

Eye contact is how you turn a monologue into a series of one-on-one conversations.
- **Don't scan or sweep.** Instead, land on **one person, finish a thought with
  them, then move to another.** Three to five seconds each.
- Spread your attention across the whole room — left, right, front, back — so
  everyone feels seen.
- **Large audience?** You can't meet every eye — pick a handful of friendly faces
  in different zones and rotate. To everyone nearby, it looks like eye contact.
- Eye contact also calms *you*: it turns a faceless crowd into individual humans.

## Movement — purposeful, not pacing

- **Nervous pacing** (drifting back and forth, rocking, swaying) is distracting
  and leaks anxiety. Plant your feet when you're not intentionally moving.
- **Purposeful movement** is powerful: move to a new spot to signal a new
  section, step *toward* the audience for an important or intimate point, use the
  stage to map your ideas ("the past was over here… the future is over here").
- Rule of thumb: **move on transitions, plant on key points.**

## Facial expression

Your face should match your message. A genuine **smile** (where appropriate) is
disarming and warm — it relaxes both you and the audience. Let your face react to
your own content: surprise, concern, delight. A frozen or anxious face
contradicts even the best words. **Congruence** — face, voice, and words all
saying the same thing — is what reads as authentic.

---

## The nervous-tell checklist

Watch your recordings for these and pick **one** to fix at a time:
- [ ] Swaying / rocking / weight-shifting
- [ ] Pacing without purpose
- [ ] Hands in pockets or hidden
- [ ] Fidgeting (clicker, ring, hair, notes)
- [ ] Closed posture (crossed arms, turned away, hunched)
- [ ] No eye contact (staring at slides, notes, floor, or ceiling)
- [ ] Frozen, tense face
- [ ] Touching face / neck

Don't try to fix all of these at once — you'll look robotic. Fix one per
practice round until it becomes natural, then move to the next.

---

## A note on authenticity

The goal is **not** to choreograph every move. Over-managed body language looks
stiff and fake. The goal is to *remove the nervous tells* that distract, and
*add* purposeful gesture and movement — so your natural, confident self comes
through. When you're genuinely engaged with your message and focused on the
audience (not yourself), good body language largely takes care of itself.

---

## 🎯 This module's exercises

1. **The mute test:** Record a 2-minute talk. Watch it with **no sound.** Would a
   stranger think this person is confident? What's the most distracting thing your
   body does? Write it down.
2. **Fix one tell:** Re-record, consciously fixing that one habit. Compare.
3. **Gesture practice:** Give a talk and deliberately illustrate three things with
   your hands (a size, a contrast, a sequence). Return to home base between each.
4. **Eye-contact drill:** With a small live audience (or even three objects placed
   around a room), practice landing a full thought on one "person" before moving
   to the next.
