# Module 5 — Slides & Visual Aids

> *"Death by PowerPoint" is a real cause of audience death — of attention.*

Slides are optional. **You** are the presentation; slides are, at most, a
supporting visual aid. The default failure mode is building a document and reading
it aloud. This module keeps your visuals in their proper place: reinforcing your
message, never replacing you.

**Watch:** Hans Rosling, *"The best stats you've ever seen"* — the gold standard
for making visuals and data genuinely thrilling (see [videos](../resources/videos.md)).

---

## First: do you even need slides?

Some of the most powerful talks have **none** — a person and a microphone (see The
Moth, or many TED talks). Slides help when you need to:
- Show something that must be **seen** (an image, a chart, a demo, a product).
- Display **data** that's clearer visually than spoken.
- Give a wandering audience a visual anchor.

If your content doesn't need any of that, consider going slide-free. Never make
slides just because a slot "expects" them.

---

## The prime directive

> **The audience should listen to YOU. Slides support what you say — they are not
> what you say.**

Every slide problem traces back to violating this. If people are *reading* your
slide, they're not *listening* to you. Human brains can't fully read and listen
at the same time — so a text-heavy slide forces a choice, and you lose.

---

## The rules of good slides

### 1. One idea per slide
Each slide makes a single point. If a slide has two ideas, split it into two.

### 2. Minimal text
- **No paragraphs. No bullet dumps.** If your slide is your script, you've built
  a teleprompter, not a visual aid.
- Aim for a headline and, at most, a few words. Some pros cap it at ~6 words per
  slide. A single strong sentence or phrase is often ideal.
- **Never read your slides aloud.** The audience can read faster than you can
  talk. Your spoken words should *add* to the slide, not duplicate it.

### 3. Big, bold visuals
- One **full-bleed image** that evokes emotion beats a page of text.
- Make text large — if you're unsure, it's too small. Assume someone's watching on
  a phone or from the back row.

### 4. Data, simplified
- One chart = one message. Strip everything that doesn't serve it (chartjunk,
  extra gridlines, 3D effects, legends you can replace with direct labels).
- **Highlight** the one number or trend you want them to see. Guide their eye.
- Say what the chart *means*, don't just show it. "The takeaway here is…"

### 5. Consistent, clean design
- Pick a simple template and stick to it: 1–2 fonts, a small color palette,
  consistent placement. Visual consistency reads as competence.
- Embrace **white space.** Cramped slides feel stressful; open ones feel
  confident.
- High contrast (dark text on light, or light on dark). Test legibility from
  across the room.

### 6. Progressive disclosure
If you must show a list or a build, reveal it **one item at a time** so the
audience is with you, not reading ahead. Don't dump a full bullet list at once.

## The "presenter, not reader" test

Ask of every deck: *"If I removed all my slides, would my talk still make sense?"*
It should. The talk lives in **you**; the slides are illustrations in the margin.
If the talk collapses without the slides, you've become a narrator for your
document — rebuild it so the ideas live in your spoken words.

---

## Practical mechanics

- **A black/blank slide is a feature.** When you want all eyes on *you* (a story,
  a key point), go to a black slide (press **B** in PowerPoint, or add a blank
  one). No visual = full attention on the speaker.
- **Rehearse *with* your slides**, using the clicker, so transitions are smooth
  and you never turn your back to read the screen. Talk to the audience, glance at
  the slide.
- **Have a no-slides backup plan.** Projectors fail. If you can give the talk
  without the deck, a tech failure becomes a shrug, not a disaster.
- **Handouts:** if there's detailed reference material, put it in a handout given
  *after*, not on a slide. Don't cram the reference doc onto the screen.

---

## 🎯 This module's exercises

1. **The 5-slide challenge:** Build a 5-slide deck for a 3–5 minute talk. Hard
   rule: **no slide may have more than 6 words or more than one image.** It'll
   feel uncomfortably sparse. Good.
2. **Rehearse talking *to* the slides,** not reading them. Record it. Check: are
   you facing the audience, or the screen?
3. **The removal test:** Give the same talk with the slides turned off. Does it
   still hold together? If not, your ideas are living on the slides instead of in
   you — fix that.
4. **Simplify a chart:** Take any busy chart and strip it to one message with one
   highlighted point.
