# Module 0 — Foundations & Mindset

> *"There are two types of speakers: those who get nervous and those who are
> liars."* — Mark Twain (attributed)

Before technique, mindset. This module reframes what public speaking actually is
and dismantles the beliefs that hold most people back.

---

## The one belief that changes everything

Most people think public speaking is a **performance** to be judged. The experts
treat it as an **act of service**: you have something useful, and you're giving
it to people who need it.

This flip matters enormously:
- Performance mindset → "How am I doing? Do they like me?" → self-focused → nerves.
- Service mindset → "Are they getting what they came for?" → audience-focused → calm.

Every time nerves rise, return to one question: **"What do these people need from
me right now?"** Attention on the audience leaves no room for attention on
yourself.

---

## Why we fear it (and why that's normal)

The fear of public speaking (glossophobia) is nearly universal. It's not a
character flaw — it's ancient wiring. To our ancestors, being watched by a group
meant possible judgment and exclusion, which meant danger. Your body still runs
that old program: racing heart, dry mouth, shaky hands.

Two things to know:
1. **The audience cannot see your nerves nearly as much as you feel them.** What
   feels like a 9/10 internal panic reads as a calm 2/10 on the outside.
2. **The physical arousal of fear and excitement is identical.** Same racing
   heart, same energy. The only difference is the story you tell about it. We
   cover this in depth in [Module 7](07-nerves-and-mindset.md).

## The myth of the "natural"

The single most damaging idea in this field: *"Great speakers are born, not
made."*

They aren't. Every speaker you admire was once bad at it and got good through
reps. Warren Buffett was so terrified of public speaking he avoided it — then
took a Dale Carnegie course and calls that diploma the most important one he owns.
Speaking is a **skill**, and skills are trainable. That's the entire premise of
this project.

---

## What "expert" actually looks like

Being an expert presenter is **not**:
- Never being nervous
- Being loud, slick, or extroverted
- Sounding like a TV anchor
- Memorizing a perfect script

It **is**:
- Being **clear** — the audience leaves knowing your one core idea
- Being **connected** — it feels like a conversation, not a recital
- Being **yourself** — your authentic voice, amplified, not an impression of someone else
- Being **useful** — they're glad they gave you their time

Introverts make superb speakers. Quiet, thoughtful delivery can be more powerful
than booming charisma. There is no one "right" style — there's only the authentic
version of *you*, made clear and intentional.

## The learning model for the whole program

You'll improve through a simple loop, repeated weekly:

```
   PREPARE  →  PERFORM  →  RECORD  →  REVIEW  →  ADJUST
      ↑                                            │
      └────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

- **Prepare** a short talk (structure it, don't script it).
- **Perform** it out loud, on your feet.
- **Record** it — your phone is your coach.
- **Review** it against the [rubric](../practice/self-assessment-rubric.md).
- **Adjust** one thing. Just one. Then loop.

The people who improve are not the ones who read the most. They're the ones who
run this loop the most times.

## 🎯 This module's exercises

1. **Watch:** Julian Treasure, *"How to speak so that people want to listen"*
   (see [videos](../resources/videos.md)).
2. **Write** (5 min): Finish these sentences honestly —
   - "When I imagine giving a talk, I'm most afraid that ___."
   - "The kind of speaker I want to become is ___."
   Keep this. Re-read it at Week 12.
3. **Record your baseline:** a **2-minute talk** on something you know well.
   Don't over-prepare. Save the video. This is your "before" picture.
