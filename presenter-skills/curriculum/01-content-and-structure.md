# Module 1 — Content & Structure

> *"If you can't write your message in a sentence, you can't say it in an hour."*
> — Dianna Booher

Delivery gets the glory, but **content and structure decide whether a talk
lands**. A brilliantly delivered talk about nothing is forgettable. A clearly
structured talk delivered plainly can change minds. This is the highest-leverage
module for most people.

---

## Step 1: Find the ONE thing

Before anything else, force yourself to answer:

> **"If the audience remembers only one sentence tomorrow, what should it be?"**

Write that sentence down. This is your **core message** (some call it the
"throughline" or "big idea"). Everything in the talk either supports it or gets
cut.

The most common mistake by far is trying to say too much. Five points delivered
means zero points remembered. **One clear idea, well supported, beats five ideas
crammed in.** When in doubt, cut.

---

## Step 2: Know your audience

Answer these before you build anything:
- **Who are they?** What do they already know? What do they care about?
- **What do they want** from this talk — and what do *you* want them to do,
  think, or feel afterward?
- **What's the gap** between where they are now and where your core message takes
  them?

Your talk is a bridge across that gap. If you don't know both banks, you can't
build it.

---

## Step 3: Choose a structure

Don't invent structure from scratch. Steal a proven shape:

### The classic three-act
- **Beginning (~15%):** Hook + tell them why this matters + preview the core idea.
- **Middle (~70%):** 2–4 points that build your case. Each point = a claim +
  evidence + a story or example.
- **End (~15%):** Restate the core message, then a memorable close / call to action.

### The rule of three
The mind loves triads: *"life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness."* Structure
your body around **three** supporting points. Three is enough to feel complete,
few enough to remember.

### Problem → Solution
1. Here's a problem you have (make them feel it).
2. Here's why it persists / why easy fixes fail.
3. Here's the solution and what it makes possible.
Ideal for pitches, proposals, and persuasive talks.

### The "sparkline" (Nancy Duarte)
Alternate between **"what is"** (the current reality) and **"what could be"** (the
better future). This tension is the engine of the greatest speeches — it's the
pattern behind MLK's "I Have a Dream." End on the "new bliss." Watch her TED talk
in [videos](../resources/videos.md).

### Story spine (for narrative talks)
*Once upon a time… every day… until one day… because of that… until finally… and
ever since then…* See [Module 4](04-storytelling.md).

---

## Step 4: Nail the opening

You have **~30 seconds** to earn attention. Never open with "Um, hi, thanks for
having me, so today I'm going to talk about…" — that's dead air.

Strong openings:
- **A story** — drop them into a moment. ("It was 2 a.m. and the pager went off…")
- **A surprising fact or statistic** — create a "wait, what?"
- **A provocative question** — one they'll turn over in their mind.
- **A bold statement** — a claim they didn't expect.
- **A vivid image** — paint one picture.

Whatever you choose: make it about **them**, not about your agenda.

---

## Step 5: Nail the closing

The ending is what they carry out the door. Never trail off with "…so, yeah,
that's it, any questions?" Design the last 30 seconds deliberately:
- **Return to your opening** (a "callback") — it creates a satisfying loop.
- **Restate the core message** in its sharpest form.
- **End with a clear call to action** or a single resonant image/line.
- Then **stop.** Land it and be silent. Don't ramble past your own ending.

---

## Step 6: Script vs. notes — speak, don't read

**Do not write a word-for-word script and read it.** Read speech sounds read. It
kills eye contact and connection.

Instead:
1. Structure your talk as a **short outline of keywords / bullet points** — the
   *ideas* in order, not the exact words.
2. Rehearse out loud until the flow is in your body. You'll say it slightly
   differently each time — that's good; it keeps it alive.
3. On the day, speak from **a few keywords on an index card** or a minimal slide.

Exception: a memorized, polished *opening* and *closing* line are worth locking
in word-for-word, because those are the highest-stakes moments.

---

## 🎯 This module's exercises

1. **The one-sentence test:** Take a topic you might present. Write its core
   message in a single sentence. Rewrite it three times, sharper each time.
2. **Structure a 3-minute talk:** One core message + exactly three supporting
   points, each with one example. Write it as an outline of **keywords only** —
   no full sentences.
3. **Speak it aloud** on your feet, from the keyword outline. Record it. Notice
   where you got lost — that's where your structure is weak, not your memory.
